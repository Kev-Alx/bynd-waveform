## 1. Understand the Original Research

Before proposing improvements, thoroughly analyze the paper and its implementation, if available.

Identify:

- The core problem, task definition, and research objective.
- The central methodological innovation and the assumptions it relies on.
- The model architecture, learning objective, training procedure, data sources, and evaluation protocol.
- The reasons the proposed method might work.
- Its potential theoretical, architectural, computational, and data-related limitations.
- Which components are essential to the core idea and which are merely implementation choices.
- The potential sources of performance bottlenecks and failure modes.

Establish a reliable baseline by reproducing the original method, or by verifying the provided baseline results if reproduction is infeasible.

Do not assume the original methodology is optimal. Critically examine whether its fundamental design choices are appropriate for the problem.

## 2. Explore Fundamentally Different Research Directions

Generate a diverse set of potential improvements. Do not restrict yourself to modifications of the original architecture.

Explore the following directions where relevant:

**A. Alternative model architectures**

- Different model families, such as Transformers, state-space models, CNNs, graph neural networks, or hybrid models.
- Alternative backbone architectures or entirely different model designs.
- Encoder-only, encoder-decoder, decoder-only, Siamese, dual-encoder, or multi-branch architectures.
- Models pretrained on relevant data or domains.
- Architectures that exploit the structure, modality, temporal properties, or relationships inherent in the task.

**B. Alternative learning paradigms**

- Supervised, self-supervised, semi-supervised, weakly supervised, and unsupervised learning.
- Contrastive learning, metric learning, masked modeling, distillation, and representation learning.
- Transfer learning, domain adaptation, continual learning, or meta-learning.
- Generative approaches, discriminative approaches, or combinations of both.
- Different optimization targets or formulations of the original task.

**C. Alternative training objectives and algorithms**

- Replace the original loss function with a fundamentally different objective.
- Reformulate the task as classification, regression, ranking, retrieval, generation, structured prediction, or another suitable formulation.
- Investigate curriculum learning, hard-example mining, sample reweighting, or alternative optimization strategies.
- Explore multi-stage training, teacher-student learning, and joint training when justified by the problem.

**D. Alternative data strategies**

- Find additional public datasets, relevant pretrained data, or complementary data sources.
- Investigate whether the task would benefit from more data, higher-quality data, different data distributions, or additional modalities.
- Explore data augmentation, synthetic data generation, pseudo-labeling, active learning, data filtering, and curriculum construction.
- Investigate alternative tokenization, feature extraction, input representations, sampling strategies, or preprocessing pipelines.
- Test whether combining datasets or transferring knowledge across domains improves generalization.

**E. Alternative problem formulations**

- Challenge the original task formulation and its implicit assumptions.
- Investigate whether the problem can be decomposed into subtasks or solved through a different computational paradigm.
- Explore retrieval-augmented methods, graph-based reasoning, iterative refinement, ensembles, or test-time adaptation when appropriate.
- Consider whether a simpler or more computationally efficient approach could achieve better results.

**F. New methodological ideas**

- Propose at least three approaches that substantially depart from the original method.
- Derive new ideas by combining relevant findings from other research areas.
- Explain why each approach might work, which weakness it addresses, and under what conditions it might fail.
- Prioritize novel, testable hypotheses over speculative architectural complexity.

These directions are a search space, not a mandatory checklist. Choose the ones that are genuinely appropriate for the paper's problem.

## 3. Conduct Research Before Experimenting

Investigate relevant prior work, including recent papers, competing methods, established baselines, and available implementations.

Determine:

- Which alternative methods have already been tested on this problem.
- Which methods have demonstrated strong results on related tasks.
- What limitations remain unresolved.
- Whether a proposed improvement is genuinely different from existing work.
- Which ideas are feasible within the available compute, data, and time constraints.

Use credible sources and verify important claims. Do not invent papers, results, citations, or novelty claims.

For every promising direction, state a falsifiable hypothesis, for example:

"If the current method struggles to capture long-range dependencies, replacing its backbone with architecture X should improve performance on examples requiring long-context information, while potentially increasing computational cost."

Explain the mechanism behind each hypothesis and define what experimental result would support or reject it.

## 4. Design a Diverse Experimental Plan

Create an experiment matrix that compares genuinely different approaches.

Include:

- The original baseline.
- Strong existing baselines from relevant literature.
- At least three substantially different methodological alternatives, subject to resource constraints.
- Data and training strategy alternatives.
- Carefully selected ablation studies to identify why an improvement occurs.

For each experiment, specify:

1. The hypothesis.
2. The methodological change.
3. The model architecture and learning paradigm.
4. The data and preprocessing requirements.
5. The training procedure and loss function.
6. The evaluation metrics.
7. The compute requirements and expected runtime.
8. The result that would support or reject the hypothesis.

Prioritize experiments by expected scientific value, probability of success, cost, and implementation difficulty.

Do not spend the entire budget on small hyperparameter searches. Allocate a meaningful portion of the experimental budget to different architectures, learning paradigms, and data strategies.

## 5. Implement and Train the Promising Approaches

When code and compute access are available, move beyond recommendations and conduct actual experiments.

- Inspect the repository, dependencies, data pipeline, training scripts, and evaluation code.
- Preserve a working copy of the original implementation.
- Implement the most promising alternative approaches as reproducible, independent experiments.
- Train the models using the available data and compute budget.
- Use appropriate validation procedures, checkpointing, and early stopping.
- Track random seeds, configurations, training time, resource usage, and experimental results.
- Verify that all methods use fair and comparable data splits and evaluation protocols.
- Diagnose training instability, overfitting, data leakage, and implementation errors.
- Run additional experiments based on observed results rather than blindly following the initial plan.

Do not fabricate completed experiments. Clearly distinguish between experiments that were actually executed, experiments that failed, and experiments that are merely proposed.

If compute or data access is insufficient, implement what can reasonably be tested and provide executable code and precise instructions for the remaining experiments.

## 6. Evaluate Scientific Validity, Not Just Performance

Evaluate every promising approach using the paper's primary metrics and relevant secondary metrics.

Also consider:

- Statistical variability across random seeds.
- Performance on different subsets and difficult cases.
- Generalization to unseen data or distribution shifts.
- Data efficiency and training stability.
- Inference latency, memory consumption, and computational cost.
- Whether improvements are robust or attributable to chance.
- Whether the method depends on additional data or pretrained models unavailable to the baseline.

Use ablations to determine which methodological changes actually contribute to improvements.

Do not claim that a method is better based on a single favorable run, an invalid comparison, or an improperly tuned baseline.

## 7. Iterate Based on Experimental Evidence

After the first round of experiments:

1. Analyze why each approach succeeded or failed.
2. Identify patterns in the results.
3. Revisit the original paper's assumptions in light of the evidence.
4. Generate new hypotheses informed by the observed behavior.
5. Combine promising ideas when scientifically justified.
6. Run targeted follow-up experiments.
7. Stop pursuing directions that repeatedly fail to justify their cost.

The goal is not to accumulate modifications. The goal is to discover a better method through evidence-driven research.

## 8. Final Deliverables

Produce the following outputs:

**A. Research analysis**

- Summary of the original method and its main limitations.
- Literature review of relevant alternative approaches.
- A ranked list of promising research directions and their rationale.

**B. Experimental report**

- A comparison table of the baseline and all tested alternatives.
- Actual measured results, including variance where available.

**C. Improved implementation**

- Reproducible source code for the best-performing method.
- Training and evaluation scripts.
- Configuration files and dependency requirements.
- Instructions to reproduce the results.
- Checkpoints or saved artifacts if feasible.

**D. Final proposed methodology**

- A clear description of the best improved method.
- Its key differences from the original paper.
- Evidence supporting the proposed improvements.
- Relevant ablation results.
- Limitations, unresolved questions, and next experiments.

**E. Research contribution assessment**

- Explain whether the improvement is incremental or potentially a substantial methodological contribution.
- Compare it against relevant existing work.
- Identify what further evidence would be required to support a publishable claim.
- Do not claim novelty without sufficient literature verification.

## 9. Non-Negotiable Principles

- Do not default to hyperparameter tuning, adding attention layers, changing hidden dimensions, or swapping individual modules.
- Treat fundamentally different architectures, learning paradigms, data sources, and task formulations as first-class candidates.
- Do not preserve the original method merely because it is already implemented.
- Do not assume a more complex model is a better model.
- Do not fabricate results or describe unexecuted experiments as completed.
- Do not leak test-set information into model selection.
- Do not ignore compute budgets, data licensing, reproducibility, or fair comparisons.
- Make decisions based on experimental evidence, not architectural intuition alone.
- Prefer a small number of informative, well-designed experiments over a large number of poorly controlled experiments.
